# Do companies pay for what they say they value?

*Part 3 summary: the Organizational Authenticity Index, 48 large S&P 500 companies, 2016 to 2024*

## What we measured
Companies state their values on their websites: customers first, integrity, safety, diversity, innovation. Each year their proxy statements also report what executives' bonuses depend on.

We compared the two. For each company and year, the **authenticity score** asks: **of everything a company emphasizes on its values page, how much is also something its executives are paid to deliver?**
- **1** means every value the page stresses appears among the bonus criteria.
- **0** means none does.
- Values a page puts front and center count more than passing mentions.

A stricter version counts a value as fully backed only when at least 10% of the bonus rides on it.

**48 of the 50 companies have a score.**
- **Apple** has no values page.
- **Berkshire Hathaway** ties no executive pay to performance criteria. Warren Buffett receives no bonus and no stock awards, and other officers' bonuses are discretionary. That is itself a finding.

## What we found
1. **The typical company backs about two-fifths of what it says.** The average score is **0.42**, and **0.34** on the stricter test. Many non-financial goals appear in bonus plans only as unweighted "factors the committee considers", with no money attached.
2. **Companies differ more from each other than they change over time.** Rankings cover the 45 companies with at least five years of data.
   - **Highest:** JPMorgan Chase (0.92), Valero (0.82) and Microsoft (0.82). Their executive scorecards name the same stakeholders their pages do.
   - **Lowest:** Charles Schwab (0.02), NVIDIA (0.03) and Salesforce (0.09). Their pages stress customers, innovation and community (NVIDIA's, innovation above all). Schwab's and NVIDIA's bonuses depended only on financial results in every year.
3. **Energy companies and banks score highest; consumer and tech companies lowest.**
   - Energy averages 0.53, Financials 0.53, Consumer Discretionary 0.31 and Technology 0.33.
   - Energy pay plans routinely include safety targets (92% of company-years) and environmental targets (72%), and most bank plans (62%) include risk and conduct. That is what these companies say they value.
4. **There is no overall trend, but not because nothing changed.**
   - Yearly averages stay between 0.36 and 0.47. Part of the 2024 high (0.47) is a correction: 2024 now counts the following year's proxy, as every other year does. It read 0.40 without it.
   - **Pay did change.** Environmental bonus criteria rose from 6% to 55% of company-years, and diversity criteria from about a fifth to over half.
   - **But pages added the same themes at the same time**, so the ratio held. When a page states environmental values, pay rewarded them in 68% of cases by 2024, up from 20% in 2016.
   - **Three shifts offset that gain:**
     - more page emphasis on community, which pay almost never rewards;
     - less emphasis on shareholders, which pay always rewards;
     - a dip around 2021 in how often pay covered stated customer values.
5. **Some backing is token. Microsoft is the clearest case.**
   - Its scorecard names customers, employees, diversity, innovation and conduct, but little money rides on each. In 2019, customers carried 16% of the bonus and the others 4% to 8% each.
   - On the basic score Microsoft ranks **3rd of 42**. It ranks **11th** if a value needs 10% of the bonus to count fully, and **19th** at 20%.
   - Thermo Fisher (15th to 34th) and SLB (18th to 32nd) follow the same pattern.
6. **About one in five non-financial areas covered by a bonus never appears on the values page.** Diversity is the most common case, then employee, conduct and safety goals. We call this "doing without saying" (Part 4).

## What the score can see, and what it cannot
**It sees real changes in pay.** ExxonMobil's pages have long mentioned the environment. Once energy-transition goals entered its bonus (one criterion in its 2021 proxy, four from 2022), the score turned that statement from unbacked to backed.

**It cannot judge what the pay rewards.** Wells Fargo scores well (0.75), but its history shows the limit:
- **Before the 2016 fake-accounts scandal,** the bonus plan's one customer goal was "deep and enduring relationships with our customers": the cross-selling target behind the misconduct. The score counts that as customer-backed.
- **In the scandal year,** the board denied the former CEO and the former head of the retail bank their bonuses, made them forfeit unvested stock, and pay was tied to financial results alone.
- **After the scandal,** risk and conduct grew to 44% of the bonus.

**The score shows when values and pay name the same stakeholder. It cannot tell whether pay rewards serving that stakeholder or exploiting them.**

## How to use this
- **Investors and analysts:** treat the score as a screening question, not a verdict.
  - A low score asks whether anything in management's incentives reinforces the stated values.
  - A big gap between the basic and strict scores means commitments sit on the scorecard with little money behind them.
- **Boards:** the per-value breakdown lists stated values with no incentive behind them. Each is either a candidate for the scorecard or a sign the page overstates.
- **Researchers:** rankings are stable across thresholds (rank correlations 0.86 to 0.98).
  - With only company-disclosed weights the correlation is 0.79, or 0.95 without Intel and SLB.
  - Compare within industries.

## What the score is not
- **Not a measure of honesty.** Paying executives only for financial results scores low by design, even if a company pursues its values in other ways. Schwab, NVIDIA and Home Depot did so every year. The score measures one visible, costly channel: executive incentives.
- **Limited to top executives and to what proxies disclose.** Most firms disclose no exact weights for non-financial goals, so the strict version partly relies on estimates.
- **2024's large companies only.** Firms that left the index, perhaps those with the widest gaps, are not in the sample.
- **Read by an AI model and spot-checked by hand.** Single company-years can be noisy; multi-year averages are more reliable.

## Bottom line
Large US companies back about 40% of what they say they value with executive pay, and about a third once only meaningful bonus weight counts. The ratio held steady from 2016 to 2024 even as environmental and diversity goals entered pay, because the pages broadened too. The useful signal is the detail: which stated values have money behind them, and which sit on the scorecard in name only.
