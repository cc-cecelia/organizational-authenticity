# Do companies pay for what they say they value?

*Part 3 summary: the Organizational Authenticity Index, 48 large S&P 500 companies, 2016 to 2024*

## What we measured
Every company describes its values on its website: customers first, integrity, safety, diversity, innovation. Every year it also tells shareholders, in its proxy statement, what its executives' bonuses depend on.

We compared the two. For each company and year, the **authenticity score** answers one question: **of everything a company emphasizes on its values page, how much is also something its executives are paid to deliver?**
- A score of **1** means every value the page stresses appears among the bonus criteria.
- A score of **0** means none does.
- Values a page puts front and center count more than passing mentions.

We also ran a stricter version that only counts a value as backed when a meaningful share of the bonus, at least 10%, rides on it.

## What we found
1. **The typical company backs about two-fifths of what it says.**
   - The average score is **0.41**.
   - On the stricter test, where a value counts only if real bonus money rides on it, the average drops to **0.34**.
   - Many non-financial goals appear in bonus plans only as unweighted "factors the committee considers", not as targets with money attached.
2. **Companies differ more from each other than they change over time.**
   - Scores range from near 0 to above 0.9, and a company's score is fairly stable from year to year.
   - Highest: **JPMorgan Chase (0.92), Microsoft (0.80), BlackRock (0.71)**. They run executive scorecards that name the same stakeholders their values pages name: customers, employees, risk and conduct.
   - Lowest: **Charles Schwab (0.02), NVIDIA (0.03), Alphabet (0.12)**. Their bonuses depend only on financial results, while their pages talk about customers, innovation and community.
3. **Energy companies and banks score highest, consumer and tech companies lowest.**
   - Energy averages 0.53 and Financials 0.52; Consumer Discretionary averages 0.30 and Technology 0.32.
   - Energy pay plans routinely include safety targets (92% of company-years) and environmental targets (72%). Most bank plans (61%) include risk and conduct. That is what those companies say they value.
4. **There is no trend from 2016 to 2024.**
   - Average scores stay between 0.36 and 0.46 every year.
   - The overall gap between stated values and pay did not close during these years.
5. **Some backing is token.**
   - Thermo Fisher, SLB and Microsoft all list many stakeholder goals in their bonus plans, but little money rides on each.
   - Their rankings fall sharply once a value must carry a fifth of the bonus to count as fully backed. Microsoft, for example, drops from 4th to 20th.
6. **About one in five of the non-financial areas a bonus covers is never mentioned on the company's values page.**
   - Diversity is the most common case, followed by employee, conduct and safety goals: firms pay for them without stating them as values.
   - We call this "doing without saying" and explore it in Part 4.

## A case that shows what the score can and cannot see
**Wells Fargo** scores well overall (0.69), but its history shows the limit of the measure. Its last bonus plan before the 2016 fake-accounts scandal did include a customer goal, "deep and enduring relationships with our customers": the cross-selling target behind the misconduct.

The score counts that as customer-backed. The pattern behind it was:
- **Before the scandal,** the customer goal in the bonus plan was the cross-selling target itself.
- **In the scandal year,** the board denied the former CEO and the former head of the retail bank their 2016 bonuses and had them forfeit unvested stock, and the bonus criteria shrank to financial results alone.
- **After the scandal,** risk and conduct grew to 44% of the bonus.

**The score shows when a company names the same stakeholder in its values and its pay. It cannot tell whether the pay rewards serving that stakeholder or exploiting them.**

## How to use this
- **Investors and analysts:** use the score as a screening question, not a verdict.
  - A low score means "ask whether anything in management's incentives reinforces these stated values".
  - A large gap between the basic and the strict score means "the commitments are on the scorecard but carry little money".
- **Boards and compensation committees:** the per-value breakdown shows which stated values have no incentive behind them. Each one is either a candidate for the scorecard or a sign that the values page overstates.
- **Researchers:** the score is a usable panel measure, robust to reasonable choices of the threshold. Compare companies within the same industry, and pair it with the stricter version.

## What the score is not
- **It is not a measure of honesty.** A company that deliberately pays executives only for financial results (NVIDIA, Schwab, Alphabet) scores low by design, even if it pursues its stated values through other means. The score measures whether stated values enter executive incentives, one visible and costly channel.
- **It covers only top executives' pay, and only what the proxy discloses.** Most companies disclose no exact weights for non-financial goals, so the strict version partly relies on estimates.
- **It sees 2024's large companies only.** Firms that fell out of the index, perhaps those with the widest gaps, are not in the sample.
- **Both sides were read by an AI model** and spot-checked by hand. Single company-years can be noisy; multi-year averages are more reliable.

## Bottom line
Large US companies typically back about 40% of what they say they value with executive pay, and closer to a third once only meaningful bonus weight counts. The gap reflects each company's pay philosophy and, to a lesser degree, its industry; it is not a trend that is closing. The most useful signal is not the level but the detail: which stated values have money behind them, and which are on the scorecard in name only.
