# Do companies pay for what they say they value?

*Part 3 summary: the Organizational Authenticity Index, 48 large S&P 500 companies, 2016 to 2024*

## What we measured
Every company describes its values on its website: customers first, integrity, safety, diversity, innovation. Every year it also tells shareholders, in its proxy statement, what its executives' bonuses depend on.

We compared the two. For each company and year, the **authenticity score** answers one question: **of everything a company emphasizes on its values page, how much is also something its executives are paid to deliver?**
- A score of **1** means every value the page stresses appears among the bonus criteria.
- A score of **0** means none does.
- Values a page puts front and center count more than passing mentions.

We also ran a stricter version that only counts a value as fully backed when a meaningful share of the bonus, at least 10%, rides on it.

**48 of the 50 companies have a score.** The other two:
- **Apple** has no values page to compare.
- **Berkshire Hathaway** ties no executive pay to performance criteria at all. Warren Buffett receives no bonus and no stock awards, and other officers' bonuses are discretionary. That is itself a finding about Berkshire's pay philosophy.

## What we found
1. **The typical company backs about two-fifths of what it says.**
   - The average score is **0.42**.
   - On the stricter test, where a value counts only if real bonus money rides on it, the average drops to **0.34**.
   - Many non-financial goals appear in bonus plans only as unweighted "factors the committee considers", not as targets with money attached.
2. **Companies differ more from each other than they change over time.** Rankings below cover the 45 companies with at least five years of data.
   - Highest: **JPMorgan Chase (0.92), Valero (0.82), Microsoft (0.82)**. Their executive scorecards name the same stakeholders their values pages name.
   - Lowest: **Charles Schwab (0.02), NVIDIA (0.03), Salesforce (0.09)**. Their pages stress customers, innovation and community (NVIDIA's, innovation above all). Schwab's and NVIDIA's bonuses depended only on financial results in every year.
3. **Energy companies and banks score highest, consumer and tech companies lowest.**
   - Energy averages 0.53 and Financials 0.53; Consumer Discretionary averages 0.31 and Technology 0.33.
   - Energy pay plans routinely include safety targets (92% of company-years) and environmental targets (72%). Most bank plans (62%) include risk and conduct. That is what those companies say they value.
4. **There is no overall trend, but not because nothing changed.**
   - Average scores stay between 0.36 and 0.47 each year. The latest year, 2024 (0.47), now also counts the following year's proxy, as every other year does. In our first run it had only one proxy and read 0.40, so part of the apparent late rise is that correction.
   - **Pay did change.** Bonus criteria on the environment rose from 6% of company-years in 2016 to 55% in 2023 to 2024. Criteria on diversity rose from about a fifth to more than half.
   - **But pages added the same themes at the same time.** The share of pages stating environmental values rose from 38% to about 60%, and the same for diversity. Those themes did become much better backed: when a page states environmental values, pay rewarded them in 20% of cases in 2016 and 68% in 2024.
   - **Three other shifts pulled the other way**, which is why the overall score stayed flat:
     - Pages put more emphasis on community, which pay almost never rewards.
     - Pages put less emphasis on shareholders, the one theme pay always rewards.
     - Pay plans covered stated customer values less often around 2021 (24%, down from 44% in 2016), before recovering.
5. **Some backing is token, and Microsoft is the clearest case.**
   - Microsoft's scorecard names many stakeholders: customers, employees, diversity, innovation, conduct. But little money rides on each. In 2019, customers carried 16% of the bonus and the others 4% to 8% each.
   - On the basic score, where any bonus criterion counts, Microsoft ranks **3rd of 42**. If a value must carry 10% of the bonus to count fully, it ranks **11th**. At 20%, it ranks **19th**.
   - Thermo Fisher (15th to 34th) and SLB (18th to 32nd) follow the same pattern.
6. **About one in five of the non-financial areas a bonus covers is never mentioned on the company's values page.**
   - Diversity is the most common case, followed by employee, conduct and safety goals: firms pay for them without stating them as values.
   - We call this "doing without saying" and explore it in Part 4.

## What the score can see, and what it cannot
**It sees real changes in pay.** ExxonMobil's pages have long mentioned the environment. Before its bonus plan included environmental goals, the score counted that statement as unbacked. Once energy-transition goals entered the bonus (one criterion in its 2021 proxy, four from 2022), the score counted it as backed.

**It cannot judge what the pay rewards.** Wells Fargo scores well overall (0.75), but its history shows the limit. The pattern:
- **Before the 2016 fake-accounts scandal,** the bonus plan's one customer goal was "deep and enduring relationships with our customers": the cross-selling target behind the misconduct. The score counts that as customer-backed.
- **In the scandal year,** the board denied the former CEO and the former head of the retail bank their 2016 bonuses and had them forfeit unvested stock, and the bonus criteria shrank to financial results alone.
- **After the scandal,** risk and conduct grew to 44% of the bonus.

**The score shows when a company names the same stakeholder in its values and its pay. It cannot tell whether the pay rewards serving that stakeholder or exploiting them.**

## How to use this
- **Investors and analysts:** use the score as a screening question, not a verdict.
  - A low score means "ask whether anything in management's incentives reinforces these stated values".
  - A large gap between the basic and the strict score means "the commitments are on the scorecard but carry little money".
- **Boards and compensation committees:** the per-value breakdown shows which stated values have no incentive behind them. Each one is either a candidate for the scorecard or a sign that the values page overstates.
- **Researchers:** company rankings are stable across materiality thresholds, with rank correlations between 0.86 and 0.98.
  - Using only the weights companies themselves disclosed, the correlation with the full version is 0.79. It rises to 0.95 without Intel and SLB, whose disclosed splits put no weight on stakeholder goals.
  - Compare companies within the same industry, and pair the basic score with the stricter version.

## What the score is not
- **It is not a measure of honesty.** A company that deliberately pays executives only for financial results scores low by design, even if it pursues its stated values through other means. Schwab, NVIDIA and Home Depot did so in every year. The score measures whether stated values enter executive incentives, one visible and costly channel.
- **It covers only top executives' pay, and only what the proxy discloses.** Most companies disclose no exact weights for non-financial goals, so the strict version partly relies on estimates.
- **It sees 2024's large companies only.** Firms that fell out of the index, perhaps those with the widest gaps, are not in the sample.
- **Both sides were read by an AI model** and spot-checked by hand. Single company-years can be noisy; multi-year averages are more reliable.

## Bottom line
Large US companies typically back about 40% of what they say they value with executive pay, and closer to a third once only meaningful bonus weight counts. The ratio held steady from 2016 to 2024 even as environmental and diversity goals entered pay, because companies' pages kept broadening at the same time. The most useful signal is not the level but the detail: which stated values have money behind them, and which are on the scorecard in name only.
